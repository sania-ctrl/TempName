### **1. Introduction** 

With the development of third-generation semiconductor devices, the high power generated by chip computing requires package materials that can serve in high-temperature environments and has excellent heat dissipation properties [1–3]. Compared with the traditional materials used for interconnections, such as thermally conductive adhesive and high-temperature Au–Sn brazing solder [4], nano-pastes have received more attention due to their unique size effect of metal nanoparticles, which can achieve low-temperature sintering and high-temperature service [5–9]. In the 1980s, nanopaste sintering technology was first applied into the field of electronic packaging [10,11], and then entered a period of vigorous development. Nanopaste, composed of metal nanoparticles as well as organic materials [12], including dispersant, binder, diluent, etc., can be divided into Cu paste [13,14], Ag paste [15,16], 

Cu@Ag core-shell paste [17–19] etc., according to the type of metal composition. Among them, silver has been studying and putting into use from an earlier stage, because of its excellent electrical and thermal conductivity, good process adaptability, easy to connect with electronic packaging materials, easy to save and other advantages [20–25]. Subbiah et al. sintered micron particle solder paste by applying a pressure of 20 MPa at 230<sup>◦</sup> C, and the shear strength of the sintered solder joint reached 70 MPa [26]. Sakamoto et al. sintered solder paste composed of 100 nm particles with 0.4 MPa at 200<sup>◦</sup> C. The strength of the solder joint reached 12 MPa after sintering [27]. Lu et al. reported a sintered paste joint with a strength of 10 MPa, in which the average particle size is 13 nm, and bonded at 150<sup>◦</sup> C without pressure. By extending the sintering time, the strength was increased to 30 MPa [28]. It can be seen that the sintering parameters, such as temperature and pressure, usually decrease as the particle size in the paste decreases, and at the same time

The exploitation of nanopaste and the investigation of joining mechanism can help improve its mechanical and thermal performance. Through molecular dynamics methods, Liu. et al. found that nanoparticles oriented by rotation to reduce the lattice mismatch and attached at the early stage of the sintering process [29,30]. Lange, A. P. et al. found that particles with different orientations have different lattice structures after sintering, and this phenomenon was also verified with in-situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) heating and observations [31,32]. Zhong et al. observed Ostwald ripening during the sintering of Cu6Sn5 particles with a 6 nm grain size [33,34]. They found that small particles at the edges of the sintered bodies gradually disappeared, while larger particles on the other side grew in size. Large nanoparticles gradually evolved into submicron or even micron-sized spheres through this mechanism during the sintering process. Li et al. further elucidated the process of nanoparticle sintering into larger sintered structures through in-situ observations of 10 nm silver nanoparticles [35]. Initially, the nanoparticles aggregated, and the grain boundaries between them were clearly visible. Subsequently, these grain boundaries gradually transformed into high-angle grain boundaries, and the overlapping regions began to contract. As the sintering time extended, many coherent twin boundaries appeared in the sintered structure, and some of these twin boundaries gradually disappeared as the sintering process continued. Eventually, the nanoparticles fused into larger submicron particles, with twin crystal structures remaining within the particles. Unlike nanoparticle sintering, the sintering of silver flakes follows a different mechanism. Due to synthetic factors, silver flake structures are generally triangular in morphology, with each triangular flake being a polycrystalline structure. Wang et al. found that due to the diffusion potential gradient and unsaturated bonds at the sharp corners of the triangular flakes, atoms at the corners are more likely to diffuse to reduce surface energy, causing the corners to contract and become rounded first. Subsequently, the edges of the silver flakes deform under high temperatures, further promoting diffusion, ultimately forming a sintered network [36]. However, these studies did not consider the diffusion mechanisms of atoms or quantitatively characterize their sintering kinetics equations. However, there is still a lack of tracking and quantitative expression of the migration mechanism of the sintering process, as well as the landmark summary of the particle attachment principle in the current mechanistic studies. 

The metal particles in the nanopaste have numerous similarities to the metal powders in the powder metallurgy, and the theory of joining has numerous points to draw upon. Coble et al. summarized that during the sintering of the two-sphere model, the atoms on the particle surface fill the junction by surface diffusion to form the sintering neck. The angle of the junction gradually flattens [37]. In order to quantitatively describe the migration mechanism of the sintering, Frenkel [38] and Kuczynski [39] derived the kinetic equation of sintered neck growth by diffusion mechanism through the ball-and-ball model. 

The connection process of nano-paste metal particles is driven by high surface energy, and the sintering neck length grows as a power function of time, which is similar to the connection mechanism in powder metallurgy. However, it is not yet clear whether the empirical parameters from powder metallurgy are fully applicable to nanoparticles. There are still gaps in the understanding of the sintering process of nanoparticles. For example, how the particles make contact, the different diffusion mechanisms under various temperature conditions, and the extent to which particle rotation observed by predecessors will lead to further sintering are all issues that remain to be studied. Therefore, in this paper, we investigate the power exponent of the empirical formula for the sintering neck growth of nanoparticles through molecular dynamics simulation and in-situ TEM heating and observation. With the help of post-processing of the simulation results, we first investigated the migration mechanism of Ag atoms and summarized the particle sintering process, and then explored the microstructure evolution of polycrystalline particles during sintering through 

simulating the sintering process of the twin-crystal particles. 

### **2. Experimental and simulation analysis methods** 

### _2.1. Introduction to the simulation method_ 

The molecular dynamics simulation of the dual-sphere model was carried out by Large-Scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) [40] software, and the simulation results were post-processed and analyzed by Open Visualization Tool (Ovito) [41] software. The Embedded atom method (EAM) [42] potential was chosen to express the interaction force between atoms, as shown in Equation (1). 



in which Ei is the total energy of atom i, Fi is the embedding energy function of the atomic electron density, rij is the distance between atoms i and j, ρij is the electron density, which is a functional specific to the types of both atoms i and j, and φij is a pair potential function. 

Before the simulation, the parameters such as surface spacing, lattice structure, heat transfer coefficient and other parameters obtained by simulation and experimental results have been well verified, which indicated the correctness of the potential function [43]. The boundary conditions were set as periodic, and the simulation space was four times the particle diameter to reduce the influence of neighboring boxes on the particles. Considering the limitations of the current computing power, small-size particle model was adopted for simulation, and the temperature accelerated dynamics (TAD) method was used for the accelerated simulation. The particle models with the diameters of 5, 10, 16, and 24 nm were established to perform the isothermal sintering simulation at 800, 900, 1000, and 1100 K. Besides, in order to compare the simulation results with the experimental results, a 40 nm diameter dual-sphere model was built to simulate the particle sintering process at 900K with the supercomputer, which was much closer to the reality and required more computing power. The model was established with energy minimization and relaxation process under NPT, and then the temperature was rapidly raised to the target temperature for long-term insulation under NVT. Then the sintering process of nanoparticles was conducted and observed. 

In order to observe the diffusion behavior of different nanoparticle atoms better, the two particles are rendered in different colors. The dislocation analysis (DXA) processing of the sintering process was carried out by Ovito software to identify different lattice structures for further observations, and the structures were identified according to the number of atomic coordinates and dyed to distinguish them. At the same time, the displacement contour diagram was used to visually represent the diffusion path of the atoms during the sintering process. The radial distribution function of all atoms, the proportion of lattice structure, the length of the sintering neck, and the potential energy function were used to quantitatively analyze the structural changes of silver nanoparticles during the low-temperature sintering process. 

### _2.2. Introduction of experimental method_ 

Nano Ag particles were synthesized via a one-step method. The silver source was 1 mM silver nitrate solution, and the reducing agent was 1 mM sodium borohydride solution, with a molar ratio of silver source to reducing agent of 1:3. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was added to the solution as a dispersant to prevent particles aggregation, at a concentration of 1 g/L. The reducing agent solution was rapidly poured into the silver nitrate solution, with stirring continuously for 10 min. To control the size of the nanoparticles, ice bath was utilized to cool the reaction system, thereby inhibiting the excessive growth of silver particles. After the reaction, the mixture was allowed to settle naturally for one day. The

### _3.1. Analysis of sintering neck growth kinetics_ 

Based on the simulation and experimental methods introduced earlier, the sintering process of nanoparticles is simulated and observed experimentally. By using molecular dynamics simulation to simulate the isothermal sintering process of single-crystal particles with different grain sizes at different temperatures, the sintering rules of nanoparticles are characterized. 

As shown in Fig. 1, molecular dynamics simulations were carried out at different temperatures for two-particle models of varying sizes, revealing that as the simulation progressed, the two particles gradually connected and formed a sintering neck at the boundary. Red and blue represents atoms of different particles, but they are both the same element. The sintering neck is the connecting part between two particles, and the length of the sintering neck is used to quantitatively characterize the sintering process. It is found that the smaller the size of the particles, the fast the particles connected. 

In order to quantitatively evaluate the degree of connectivity, the length of the sintering neck was measured, representing its growth. A complete particle sintering process was illustrated in Fig. 2(a), with the dual-spherical models in contact with each other at the beginning of sintering, and an initial sintering neck structure formed at the junction. As the sintering continued, the red and blue atoms, which represented the two particles, diffused and mixed with each other, and the sintering neck grew further. When the length of the sintering neck approached the diameter of the particle, the particles tended to form a larger particle. The variation of particle size as well as sintering neck length in the simulation model for different sizes and temperatures was measured. ### _3.2. Analysis of sintering mechanism of single crystal particles_ 

According to the above discussion, the correlation coefficients of the sintering diffusion mechanism in nanoparticles at low temperature are very different from the empirical sintering coefficients of powder 

metallurgy, which indicates that the kinetics of nanoparticle sintering are different from that of powder metallurgy. Through the postprocessing of the simulation results, it was found that the sintering process of nanoparticles was mainly divided into three stages. At the first stage, the sintering neck grew rapidly and achieved the initial connection; At the second stage, the length of the sintering neck, the angle of the sintering neck and the distance from the particles center changes slowly. The process of sintering seems to keep stable. The third stage is the rapid combination stage, the dual-sphere model fused quickly to form one single particle.

The structural analysis and displacement vector of the simulation process in 24 nm particles at 900 K is shown in Fig. 4. Taking one cycle as an example, during the first 3 ps, the temperature increased and the sintered particles got higher internal energy. As a result, the atoms vibrated vigorously and moved away from their original lattices, causing the expansion of the particles. At 3 ps, the particle expansion s reached its maximum, and most of the atoms were no longer in a long-range ordered state. The displacement vector indicated that the atoms moved uniformly towards the particle surface during expansion. At the particle junction, the atoms from both sides moved towards the sintering neck to fill it. During the contraction stage at 3–8 ps, potential energy was converted into kinetic energy, causing the shrinkage of the particles, and the atoms returned to their lattice sites and rearranged themselves. Migration towards the sphere of the atoms could be seen in the displacement vector. Sintering contraction alternated with expansion, and a large number of dislocations appeared at the sintering neck and grew rapidly to reach the initial length. It is clear that the particles underwent repeated expansion and contraction processes during the initial stage of sintering. The period is around 8–10 ps, during which the initial growth of the sintering neck forms. 

In the sintering simulation of a 10 nm diameter dual-sphere model at 700 K, as shown in supporting information figure S1 and S2, the phenomenon was found to be different, in which the contraction and expansion at the first stage was no longer obvious, which was due to the fact that the initial energy input was not enough to support a large number of atoms to detach from the lattice structure. A large number of dislocations inside the sintered neck is maintained throughout the sintering process, which shows that the energy input is not sufficient for most of the atoms to migrate when the temperature was low, and the sintering process as well as the growth of the sintering neck mainly relied on the slippage of the dislocations. 

During the sintering process, radial distribution function (RDF), atomic spacing, sintering neck length and system potential energy also changed with the contraction and expansion of the particles. As shown in Fig. 5 (a), at 0 ps, almost all the atoms were regularly arranged and the 

particles were face-centered cubic, and RDF showed narrow and high peaks, with the first peak value of 2.89 Å, which was the closest atomic spacing in the face-centered cubic structure. At 3 ps, the peaks of RDF became shorter and wider, proving that the atomic diffused away from lattice structure sites. And at 8 ps, the peaks became higher and narrower, indicating that part of the atomic spacing was restored to the face-centered cubic structure. Fig. 5 (b) shows the variation of the percentage of different structure, to be specific, FCC and OTHER. It is obvious that the two structures alternate with decreasing amplitude, and the structure tended to be stable as the sintering continued. In Fig. 5 (c), it can be seen that the potential energy also changed periodically during the sintering process of the different models, with a similar law to that of the structural changes, and the amplitude of the energy peaks decreased as the particle size decreased. Fig. 5 (d) shows the growth curves of the sintered neck lengths for different models, and it can be seen that with the expansion and contraction process of sintering, the sintered necks also oscillated between lengthening and shortening, but the overall trend was lengthening. 

It can be seen that in the initial stage of nanoparticle sintering, it relies mainly on the absorption and release of energy, the expansion and contraction of the particles and the continuous migration of atoms to achieve the initial cohesion of the particles. 

During the second stage of sintering, the particles no longer contracted and expanded rapidly, but instead rotated directionally. Fig. 6 displayed the displacement vectors of particle models with different sizes during sintering. For a more intuitive analysis, the displacements of some atoms were hidden. The particles rotated around an axis as the face-centered cubic structure atoms moved directionally within them. The axis of rotation was somewhat random, and the direction of rotation varied depending on the model used. The surface atoms migrated further than the relatively stable internal atoms, but the direction of migration was also random. It can be determined that the surface atoms were in an active state after absorbing energy and were making random long-range migrations on the surface of the particles. By combining experimental observations with molecular dynamics simulations, the analysis of the sintering process of single crystal and polycrystalline structured particles, we can conclude that the sintering mechanism of nanoparticles changes due to their high surface energy and low-temperature sintering conditions. When the temperature approaches the melting point, particles quickly melt and form connections. At lower sintering temperatures, the sintering process can be divided into three stages, as shown in Fig. 8(g). At the first stage, the energy input led to the expansion, contraction and the rearrangement of the atoms, and the sintering neck grew rapidly and achieved the initial connection. The second stage is mainly represented by the rotation of particles, and at the same time, the atoms on the surface diffused actively, making the sintering neck grew further; the third stage is the rapid combination stage, the dual-sphere model fused quickly to form one single sphere when the two spheres structure match or the energy in particles are enough to combine. Additionally, the particles remain unmelted through the whole process of sintering. 

### **4. Conclusion** 

In this paper, the sintering kinetics of Ag particles were investigated by mutual verification of in-situ heating TEM observations and molecular dynamics simulations. The sintering mechanisms of nanoparticles change with sintering time, and the sintering process can be divided into three stages according to different sintering behaviors. And the following conclusions have been reached. 

- 1) The experimental results revealed that the power exponent in the sintering neck growth kinetic equation for nanoparticles is greater than the empirical one for microparticles. The simulated power

Upon lattice matching, the particles cease rotation and enter the third stage, where they rapidly coalesce.
